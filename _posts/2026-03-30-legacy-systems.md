In 2024, the IRS still ran parts of its Individual Master File on COBOL, software first written in 1962. When Congress allocated modernisation money, roughly 70% went to keeping the old system running in parallel. The new system was a minority of the budget.

That ratio is typical. Legacy replacement isn't a project. It's a tax.

## The maintenance bill is hidden in operations

Legacy systems don't show up on a transformation slide as costs. They show up as:

- Specialist contractor rates of $300 to $600 an hour, because few people know the stack
- Emergency patches scheduled around the two or three people who understand the code
- Integration middleware that exists only to translate between the old system and everything newer

The average COBOL specialist is over 60. When they retire, the hourly rate goes up, not down.

These costs land in operations budgets, not IT budgets. Legislatures approve modernisation funding without seeing the full run rate of what the old system already costs.

## Replacement projects fail when they try to replace everything at once

The pattern is well documented. Big-bang replacements take longer than estimated, cost more than approved, and get cancelled or scaled back. The old system keeps running. The organisation ends up worse off, maintaining two systems and two skill sets.

The UK's NHS National Programme for IT tried to standardise records systems across hundreds of trusts at once. It spent £12.7 billion over a decade before being dismantled. Scale didn't reduce risk. It multiplied it.

Incremental replacement works better in almost every documented case. Wrap the old system in an API. Move one function at a time. Keep the old system as the record until the new one has proven itself for a full business cycle.

It's slower. It's also the only approach with a track record of finishing.

## The political problem is timing

Modernisation costs land on one minister's watch. The benefits land on a successor's. No one gets credit for a system that works quietly, and everyone gets blamed for an outage during a transition.

That's why legacy systems survive. Not because replacement is technically impossible, but because the political payoff arrives too late.

**Your takeaway:** Before approving any legacy replacement, demand the run rate of the current system, all-in, including contractor rates and integration middleware. If that number isn't on the table, the comparison being sold to you is incomplete.
