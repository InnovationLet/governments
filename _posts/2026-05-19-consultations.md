The EU's 2020 consultation on the AI Act drew over 1,000 responses. Analyses published after adoption found the final regulation tracked closer to input from large firms and industry associations than from civil society. Influence in consultations isn't evenly distributed, and it isn't random.

Some respondents are paid to be there. Trade associations employ full-time policy staff for consultations. A volunteer writing in on a Sunday evening is competing with that.

If you're submitting to one, you should understand how the process actually works.

## Not every consultation is a real question

Some consultations are genuine. The body doesn't know the answer and is weighing options. Others are confirmation exercises. The decision is made, and the consultation exists to build a paper trail and claim legitimacy.

You can tell the difference from the questions. If the document asks "should we do X" and provides no cost estimates, no alternative options, and a four-week window over a holiday period, the answer is probably already yes. If it asks you to compare two or three specific options with published impact assessments, someone is still deciding.

## Specific beats passionate

Consultation responses get read by analysts, not journalists. They're looking for:

- Evidence that connects a proposed rule to a measurable outcome
- Concrete examples of how the rule would work in practice
- Alternatives the drafters may not have considered
- Technical details about implementation timelines and costs

A response that's 80% argument and 20% data loses to one that's the reverse. If you can point to a specific clause, explain what it would do to a specific workflow, and propose amended language, you're in the top tier of submissions.

## Timing matters more than most people think

Early responses, in the first week, get more attention than late ones. Analysts form a working view before the deadline, and late submissions have to displace it. If the consultation runs eight weeks, submit by week two.

Also, ask for a meeting. Most consultation bodies will grant one to anyone who asks, and a 30-minute conversation does more than a 3,000-word document.

Finally, read the summary of responses when it's published, usually six to twelve months later. It shows which arguments landed and which didn't, and it's the best training material you'll get for the next consultation.

**Your takeaway:** Before writing a response, check whether the consultation publishes impact assessments and alternative options. If it doesn't, spend your time elsewhere. If it does, submit early, lead with evidence, and request a meeting in your cover note.
