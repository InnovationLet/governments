The UK's Universal Credit programme passed £10 billion in IT spending before it finished. Most of that money went to system integrators billing by the hour, under contracts that paid for effort, not outcomes. The software was almost a side effect.

That's the pattern. Governments don't have a technology problem. They have a buying problem.

## The tender is written for lawyers, not users

A typical government IT tender runs 100 to 300 pages. It specifies technologies, delivery milestones, and reporting structures in detail. It almost never describes what a citizen should be able to do on a Tuesday morning.

When the requirement is "provide a case management system," vendors respond with compliance documents. The evaluation panel scores those documents. Nobody runs the software until years into the contract.

## Fixed-price big-bang contracts concentrate risk in the wrong place

Governments like large fixed-price contracts because they look predictable. They're not. A five-year, £50 million deal with quarterly milestone payments forces vendors to overpromise at the start and bury the buyer in change requests at the end.

Smaller contracts with shorter terms do the opposite. They let buyers stop paying when software doesn't work. They also force agencies to write clearer requirements, because vague ones can't be scoped into three-month engagements.

Some things that work in practice:

- Breaking programmes into services under 12 months and £2 million
- Paying for demonstrated working software at each review, not slide decks
- Writing acceptance criteria that real users can test against
- Requiring source code and data to be escrowed, so the supplier is replaceable

## Cultural change matters more than process change

New procurement frameworks don't fix anything if the people running them are rewarded for signing large contracts. The incentive structure inside government needs to shift toward measured service outcomes and away from contract volume.

That means training procurement staff on technology, not just on process. It means letting civil servants who've built products outside government join evaluation panels. It means publishing post-project reviews without redaction, so failures are visible.

The reform isn't mysterious. It's smaller contracts, clearer outcomes, working software demos, and people on the panel who know what they're looking at.

**Your takeaway:** If you're reviewing a government tech tender, ignore the budget line first and look at the contract length and acceptance criteria. A short contract with user-testable acceptance criteria will produce better software than a long one with perfect financial projections.
