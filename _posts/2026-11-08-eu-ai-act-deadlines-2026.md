The EU AI Act deadlines are now part of the compliance calendar. The AI Act transparency rules became enforceable on August 2, 2026, and two new prohibitions take effect on December 2, 2026. High-risk obligations were pushed to December 2027 and August 2028, but the near-term work is already live.

That leaves companies with two questions. What must change before December, and which rules actually apply to their systems right now? The answers depend on what the system does, where it is sold and whether it was on the market before August. ## Which AI Act rules apply now?

The transparency rules in Article 50 apply from August 2, 2026. They cover four situations:

- An AI system that interacts with people must tell them they are dealing with a machine, unless that is obvious.
- Providers of generative AI must mark synthetic content in a machine-readable form and make it detectable.
- Deployers must disclose deepfakes as artificially generated or manipulated.
- Deployers of emotion recognition or biometric categorisation systems must inform the people exposed to them.

The Commission's AI Act Service Desk says enforcement also started on August 2 for general-purpose AI models, prohibited practices, transparency rules and AI literacy. National authorities and the AI Office share the enforcement work.

## What changes on December 2, 2026?

Two things happen on the same date.

First, two new prohibitions start. The AI Act will ban AI systems that generate or manipulate non-consensual intimate material or child sexual abuse material, where the relevant outputs are intended or reasonably foreseeable and there are no adequate safeguards. The rule covers providers that place such systems on the market and deployers that use them for those purposes.

Second, the transition period for Article 50(2) ends. Providers of AI systems that generate synthetic content and were placed on the market before August 2, 2026 have until December 2 to comply with the marking and detection obligation. The Council says the grace period was cut from six months to three during the final negotiations.

## Why did the high-risk deadlines move?

The original plan applied high-risk rules in August 2026. The standards companies needed to prove compliance were not ready, so the Commission proposed a delay. The Digital Omnibus on AI entered into force on July 27, 2026, and it set fixed dates instead of a floating trigger.

The new schedule looks like this:

| Date | What applies |
| --- | --- |
| February 2, 2025 | Prohibited practices and AI literacy rules |
| August 2, 2025 | General-purpose AI model rules and governance |
| August 2, 2026 | Most rules, including Article 50 transparency and enforcement |
| December 2, 2026 | New prohibitions and the Article 50(2) marking deadline |
| August 2, 2027 | At least one national AI regulatory sandbox per member state |
| December 2, 2027 | High-risk rules for Annex III systems |
| August 2, 2028 | High-risk rules for AI embedded in regulated products |

Annex III covers stand-alone systems used in areas such as biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration and border control. Annex I covers AI inside regulated products such as medical devices, machinery, toys and lifts.

## Who is affected by the high-risk delay?

Providers of high-risk systems get more time. The duties still apply. Recruitment software, credit scoring tools, exam scoring systems and biometric identification systems still face the full set of duties before they can be sold.

Those duties include risk management, data governance, logging, technical documentation, human oversight and accuracy testing. Public authorities also have registration duties when they deploy high-risk systems. The delay moves the deadline, and the work still has to be done.

## What changes in Northern Ireland?

The UK government published guidance on November 4, 2026, confirming that the EU AI Act applies in Northern Ireland in a specific and limited way under the Windsor Framework from November 10, 2026.

The guidance is aimed at developers, deployers, manufacturers, importers and distributors. It asks them to check whether their products are in scope and to prepare for rules on prohibited, high-risk and limited-risk systems. Great Britain does not follow the EU regime, so companies selling across the Irish Sea need to check both sets of rules.

## What is the first harmonised standard?

The Commission published the reference for EN 18286:2026 in the Official Journal on October 14, 2026. The standard covers the quality management system that providers of high-risk AI need under Article 17.

Following a harmonised standard gives a provider a presumption of conformity with the requirements it covers. EN 18286 covers one requirement. It is the first standard in a series, and the Commission says more will follow. For now, it gives high-risk providers a documented way to build the management system the Act expects.

## How large are the fines?

Transparency breaches can draw fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Breaches of the prohibited practices can reach €35 million or 7% of turnover under Article 99 of the Act.

The Commission is still writing the procedural rules for how it runs cases. Its draft implementing regulation closed for feedback on November 9, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Brussels time. The draft sets a five-year limitation period for fines, with a ten-year cap, and rules on access to the case file. It has not been adopted.

## What should companies do before December 2?

Start with a list of systems. A policy document can come later.

1. List every AI system you provide or deploy in the EU, including tools bought from vendors.
2. Mark which ones interact with people, generate content, recognise emotions or make high-risk decisions.
3. Check that chatbots and assistants disclose that users are talking to a machine.
4. Check that generated images, audio, video and text carry a machine-readable mark.
5. Add a clear label to deepfakes and to AI-generated text on matters of public interest that had no human review.
6. Confirm that older generative systems will meet the December 2 marking deadline.
7. Record the evidence. Regulators will ask how you decided a system was out of scope.

If you sell into Northern Ireland, add the UK guidance to the review and check the November 10 start date.
